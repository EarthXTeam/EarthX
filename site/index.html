Four suspects, one chain

Heat is the driver. It pulls harder on the water below it: rain is the supply, evapotranspiration is the loss, soil water is what is left. A place can have perfectly steady rain and still dry out, so each suspect is tested on its own and then read together.

How we test, and what to trust

Mann-Kendall test. It checks whether yearly values keep moving in one direction more often than chance allows. We report the Sen slope (the typical rate of change) and a p-value. Below 0.05 we call it confirmed. Visible but not yet significant is case open, with a rough estimate of the extra years of data needed. No visible direction is no evidence.

We show non-significant results on purpose: some trends only need a longer record. No forecasts and no machine learning, because a forecast cannot be checked here.

Every case runs in three steps, in this order.

  1. Mechanism. The physics that would produce a trend, before any data is on screen. You commit to a prediction.
  2. Evidence. The grid-cell map and the regional series, with the fitted trend line withheld. You inspect cells, keep a notebook, and call the verdict for the region.
  3. Reading. Now the test's own verdict, the numbers, how your two calls compared, and what would overturn the result.

The order matters. A verdict you predicted and then tested is a thing you understand; a verdict handed to you on arrival is a thing you scroll past.

Honest limits. The numbers on this site come from NASA monthly data retrieved through Giovanni, 1981–2025, averaged onto a 0.75° grid. Precipitation and temperature are observation-based; evapotranspiration and soil moisture are land-model outputs constrained by observations, not direct measurements. Neighbouring cells are not independent, and a different product or window can give a different answer, so every verdict here is a trend in this dataset — a lead worth following, not a settled fact about the region.
